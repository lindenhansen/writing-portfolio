But there is also a baby Elk in a field somewhere. And it will stay like a memory stain. Warm felted ears in my hands. An order from God. A command from the forest. Or the wind asking me to lift my sticky fingers from its path. A hoard of Aspen clones saying make way for what will be.

I am ten years old and the sun is hot. Stinging and buzzing. Sparkling across the Wyoming cattle pasture. No stock in sight. Only three wispy figures: two elk and a coyote prancing giddily in the distance. I shield my eyes from the piercing rays and watch the scene unfold.

Coyote creeps closer, eyes beady with hunger. Momma Elk freaks. She thunders away, screeching, beckoning her spawn to follow. It doesn't. Instead, it collapses under the hopeless shade of a rickety wood-post fence. Minutes pass, Carter wields a shotgun. POW. One warning and the coyote skips out. A mouthful of lean elk meat not worth the spray of metal. After the scatter, only the baby's shape remains.

The orb ablaze in the sky inches closer to afternoon. Nail-biting ensues. Hours in the sun welcome death for freshly-born beings. We watch the calf's ribs rise and fall at a leaden pace, eclipsed by the tall grass. Momma is nowhere in sight. Was she spooked by the sound of firepower?

We think to ourselves: We God-fearing few must do a thing. Place a name to it. Over every creeping thing that creepeth upon the earth. We are creators, protectors, saviors of the wild…

And then we are in the truck bed—quivering hooves sounding against its floor. We take it to a stall in the barn nearby. Feed it milk from a makeshift bottle. Warmth trickles down my arm, steaming. Sustenance breathes a spark into its bleeding black eyes, ripe with abandonment but I see peril by another name. Ten years of butterflies and bees burnt to ash by one second in those astral orbs. Cherished ideas of a perfect balance crumble quietly. My hand is the inflictor, not nature's hot sun. It is us as light melds with darkness in the flickering hint of dusk. A hole ripped in the fragile tapestry—a nakedness no one dares permit.

We stumble back home, weary and confused. Then a sight in the window makes our hearts drop to our knees. Bursting through a cloudy surface, reality strikes hard in the face. Mom is back, searching for her child, echoing painful coos. Desperately, intuitively, demandingly as stars look down on us from the heavens, asking where is their dark night.

Keys in the ignition. Back to the barn. Scoop the calf into the truck. Deliver it to the field. Pray to the skies, misty-eyed.

One second passes.

Is the reek of human stench too strong? Is there forgiveness here in this tall grass? We wait. And then it happens, they trot together, sounding mews of reunion, into the hazy yellow evening like a sigh of relief.
